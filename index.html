What Florida counties can do about a large data center

A guide to what Florida counties can do when a large data center is proposed. Pick a county and a type of project to see what Florida law allows, what each item would mean for you, and how several items add up.

  • Standards.

    Every project must meet the county's standards.

  • County item.

    The county can charge an impact fee, or use a development agreement, to cover what is needed to handle the impacts its own impact study measures, such as backup power at county facilities.

  • County land lease.

    On land the county owns, the county can also set the rent, and part of the rent can be paid in kind.

  • Private agreement.

    Other benefits, such as computing time or training seats, can come only from a private agreement between the developer and a community group. The county is not a party to that agreement.

What a data center project could provide, and where each item goes

This page is not a county document. County staff should not use it to point community groups to a developer.

Each item on this page has one home. Items the county requires go in its development agreement or an impact fee, and only to handle impacts its own impact study measures (Exhibit "B-1"). Each needs its own written record showing it is tied to those impacts and in proportion to them, because a developer can challenge any county condition in court (s. 70.45) and that right cannot be signed away. On county-owned land, the county can also set rent, partly in kind, in a lease. Everything else can come only from a private agreement between the developer and a community group; the county is not a party and does not ask for it or consider it. A county also may not promise in an agreement to approve or support a future rezoning (Morgran Co. v. Orange County, 818 So. 2d 640 (Fla. 5th DCA 2002)).

    How to use this page

    1. Pick a county and a type of project at the top of Find. The map shows which Florida counties are open to data centers, paused, or have banned them.

    2. Read the cards. Each card says in one line what the item is and who would receive it. The colored label says whether it passes the framework's test. Tap More for what it does for the community, how it is rated, and the legal details for county staff.

    3. Add items to your picks. My picks shows how they work together, what to watch out for, and, when you pick from more than one type of project, what they could add up to across your community.

    What "in-kind" means: the project provides something real and usable, like land, equipment or computing time, instead of a cash payment (in a private agreement, the computing share defaults to an annual payment, and the community group may choose the computing itself). Cash can end up in a general budget; a thing built or delivered stays where it was meant to go.

    Scenarios: a guide for planners and councils

    Scenarios let a county or a regional planning council sketch what several hypothetical projects would involve: their provisions, impacts and legal limits, what would move between them and the public, and what stands in the way. Everything in a scenario is hypothetical; a county may decline any agreement, and nothing here assumes one will be signed.

    1. Start. Choose a county in Find, or open Scenarios and pick a region under Start from a region. To work across counties, turn on Imagine a partnership under the map and tap the counties to add.
    2. Add projects. Add as many as the scenario needs. Choose the type of project and the kind of facility; the provisions offered change with it. Use Hide to collapse a project to one line, Collapse all for a whole county, and the trash icon to remove a project.
    3. Say what you know about the site. A project with the recovered heat right, the heat-supplied greenhouse or dark fiber to schools asks up to three questions: is a heat user next to the site, is there room next to it for a community greenhouse, and would its fiber route pass near a school, college, library or nonprofit clinic the county does not run. Answer yes, no or not known from what you already know, and leave not known if no site is in mind; nothing asks where a site is. The flow view marks those arrows with a check, a cross or a question mark, and the summary lists the answers. A No answer means the greenhouse or the fiber is not counted in what the provisions could provide; the heat right stays counted, as an option for a later user. The page does not rank sites, and because these items come only from private agreements, the answers are no reason to prefer one site over another.
    4. Read the flow view. Orange arrows stay near the site. Dashed orange arrows end at a county facility; nothing leaves the site. Solid blue reaches farther by route or network. Dashed blue crosses a county line, and only through an agreement: between counties for county items, and between recipients for private ones. Grey dotted arrows are items waiting on a legal answer; they are never counted.
    5. Compare with what could be. The switch above the flow view shows the scenario if every item were allowed: set-aside items and items Florida does not allow today are drawn as if allowed. Use it to show what a change in law would add. It is never saved and never in a code.
    6. Mark what is real. When an agreement for the project is actually signed, mark that project as signed. It is then kept fixed: it cannot be edited or removed until unmarked, and clearing a county or the whole scenario keeps it.
    7. Save and share. Get a scenario code saves everything in one code. One code per county gives each county its own line, so each county keeps its own record.
    8. Build a regional picture from county codes. Collect each county's code and paste them into the box, one per line, then press Add a code to this scenario. Each county in a code replaces that county's projects here, except projects marked as signed, which stay. Undo is available right after.
    9. Update over time. If a county signs a new agreement or a plan changes, update that county, mark signed projects, and issue a new code. Keep the dated codes with the plan's records; the code is a snapshot, not a ledger.
    10. Print. Print the whole scenario, or choose one county and print only that county.

    What a scenario is not. It does not read or replace a comprehensive plan or a regional plan, and it is not a forecast. It is a sketch to put next to those plans. Nothing is uploaded or sent anywhere; the scenario lives on this device and in any code you copy.

    The 8 parts of the In-Kind test

    An item "works" only if it meets all eight. A strong showing on one cannot make up for failing another.

    1. Cheap to share. It can be split off without hurting the project's own use, and it costs the giver little compared with its value to the public.
    2. Someone ready to receive it. A public body or nonprofit exists by the time any agreement is signed, with the staff and authority to run it. One exception: a low-cost item that only keeps a future option open, such as spare conduit, can count with a likely future user, as long as it carries a review or end date.
    3. Usable as is. The public can use it directly, without selling or converting it first.
    4. Tied to the project's effects. There is a specific, written link between something the project causes and what the item provides, made separately for each item. For a county item this is the legal test: the item must answer an impact the county's impact study measures. For a lease or a private agreement it is a test of fairness, not of law.
    5. Only the signer needed. Delivery cannot depend on anyone who did not sign, such as a neighboring business that may never arrive. Where it would, the developer can only promise to offer it.
    6. Not a protected service. If a utility or franchise holder has the sole right to provide the service, the item must take a built form instead, such as equipment, conduit or a larger pipe.
    7. Checkable by the public. Whoever receives it can confirm delivery with evidence it can get and understand, not only the giver's word.
    8. Lasting. It survives a sale of the facility, and it does not lose its value just because it goes unused for a while.

    Two kinds of items are left out even if they pass: help for people displaced by the project, paid for by the same company (In-Kind test 4.1: that help comes from the community's own operating surplus or public programs, never from the company), and rules about what a business does inside its own building. A company's terms for its own employees, such as advance notice or severance, are a separate question. An item that passes then goes to one home: the standards ordinance, a county development agreement or impact fee, a lease of county land, or a private agreement the county is not part of (test 1.6). Source: In-Kind Eligibility and Characterization Test v1.5, Parts 3 and 4, in plain words.

    What Florida law does not let counties require (all project types)

    Some items on this page cannot be required by a Florida county because a state law has taken the subject away from counties. A private agreement between the developer and a community group can carry some of them, such as protections for the developer's own workers, because these laws limit counties, not private parties. The county is not part of any such agreement; which county acts would draw it in is an open question (Open Legal Questions, Question 1). Others are simply not in Florida law, or are set by federal law. For each one, this shows what it would do, the law involved, the reason on record (or that none could be found), how other states handle it, and who could change it. It describes; it does not recommend a change. Effects are described by type of place, never by a named county or city.

    Words used on this page
    Trust (community computing trust)
    A nonprofit that residents form and govern to hold and run a shared computing allocation under a private agreement, so it stays out of any government budget and out of company control. The county has no seat, role or claim in it. Until a trust exists, a state college, state university or shared operator named in the agreement can hold the allocation.
    Dark fiber
    Fiber-optic cable laid but not yet in use, which the public can light for its own buildings.
    Site facts
    What a planner already knows about the area around a hypothetical project's site, answered yes, no or not known in Scenarios: whether a heat user is next to it, whether there is room next to it for a community greenhouse, and whether its fiber route passes near a school, college, library or nonprofit clinic the county does not run. The page never asks where a site is and does not rank sites.
    Colocation
    Renting space in a data center to run your own servers there.
    Impact fee credit
    When a county charges an impact fee and the developer builds or delivers something in the same category, the county subtracts its value from the fee (s. 163.31801(5)), so nothing is charged twice. The model public safety and emergency power impact fee ordinance gives this credit for emergency equipment delivered in kind.
    Standby right
    A right that only switches on when something happens, such as a declared emergency.
    Our model text
    How far this framework's own wording for an item has got, from an idea to a provision that has held up.
    Tried elsewhere?
    Whether anyone has actually done something like the item, anywhere. It is about the idea, not this framework's wording.
    In-kind
    Something real and usable, like land, equipment or computing time, given instead of money (in a private agreement, the computing share defaults to an annual payment, and the community group may choose the computing itself).
    Development agreement
    A contract between a county and a developer, adopted after public hearings, that fixes the rules for one project for up to 30 years (Florida Statutes ss. 163.3220 to 163.3243). In this framework it carries only mitigation of impacts the county's impact study measures, the county's standards as recorded promises, and a dig-once conduit term the county pays for.
    Standards ordinance
    Rules every project must follow whether or not it signs an agreement, such as noise, water and flood limits.
    Impact study (Exhibit "B-1")
    The county's own study measuring a project's impacts, such as demand on fire rescue or shelters. Every county requirement must answer an impact it measures.
    Mitigation
    What a project pays for or provides to handle an impact it causes, sized by the impact study.
    Impact fee ordinance
    A county law charging a one-time fee for the capital cost of public facilities a project needs. The model ordinance covers fire rescue, emergency medical and emergency power at county public safety and water facilities and, where its study supports it, public shelters.
    Ground lease
    A lease of county-owned land. The county acts as landowner and may set rent, partly in kind (s. 125.35).
    Baseline
    Anything the standards ordinance or the law already requires. It is never counted as a benefit, and it is never traded.
    Link to the project (nexus)
    How clearly an item answers something the project itself causes, such as extra strain on the power grid.
    In proportion (proportionality)
    An item has to be roughly the right size for the effect it answers, not more.
    Exaction
    A condition government attaches to a permit or approval. Florida lets a developer challenge one in court if it lacks a link to the project or is out of proportion (s. 70.45).
    Private agreement (community benefits agreement)
    A contract between the developer and an existing community group. It carries benefits that do not answer a measured impact, such as computing time or training. The county is not a party and does not ask for, negotiate, review or consider it.
    Vesting
    The rules in place when a development agreement is signed keep applying for its term (s. 163.3233(1)), unless, after a public hearing, the county finds that a later rule meets one of the tests in s. 163.3233(2), such as being specifically anticipated in the agreement. It is an effect of the statute, not a price paid for anything in the agreement.
    Moratorium
    A temporary pause on new applications while a county studies an issue.
    Recipient
    Whoever receives an item and reports what it got. For county mitigation, a county facility such as a shelter or fire station. For a private agreement, a community computing trust, a state college or university, or another nonprofit; never the county, a county department or another local government that acts on the project's approvals.
    Type A, B, C
    Every year (A), built or given once (B), or only in emergencies (C).
    Preemption
    When state or federal law takes a subject away from counties, so a county cannot regulate it.
